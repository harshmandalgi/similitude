\subsection{Simulated tools keep decisions}
Simulated tools were the best shortcut of group~C. For the Wikipedia agent (W1), the grounded
recipe kept 19 of 20 clear decisions and the pure simulator kept 18 of 20. The noise ceiling kept
20 of 20. For the research planner (W3), simulated researcher tools kept all 8 clear decisions, and
the second full run kept 6 of 8. In Figure~\ref{fig:tracking}a--b, the scores of the miniature
follow the scores of the full workflow ($r = 0.95$ and $0.74$).

The grounded recipe also kept the diagnosis better. The failure reasons of the judge agreed with
the full workflow at $\pi = 0.41$ for the grounded recipe and at $0.27$ for the pure simulator. The
ceiling is $0.57$. A pure simulator invents pages that do not contain the facts, so it shows more
retrieval failures. The grounded recipe also replays recorded calls, so this test does not separate
the effect of the simulator from the effect of replay.

\subsection{Reduction is cheap and safe}
The cheapest valid miniatures came from group~B. A random quarter of the tasks kept 17 of 20 clear
decisions at 0.20$\times$ the cost of the evaluation. Short tool outputs kept all 20 clear
decisions. In W2, a cheaper simulated customer kept all 13 clear
decisions, with a ranking agreement of $\tau = 0.79$. Replay of the planner and the researchers in
W3 cost 0.21$\times$ the full run and gave the same outputs for the same inputs.
Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} shows why our test did not confirm this replay miniature.

\subsection{The noise ceiling prevents a false alarm}
\label{sec:ceiling}
The writer group of W3 shows why a team must measure the noise ceiling. The replay miniature gave
each writer variant the same research. Its ranking of the writer variants disagreed with the full
workflow ($\tau = -0.47$). But the full workflow also disagreed with its own second run
($\tau = -0.05$), and only 2 of its 5 clear decisions came back. Each full run collects new
research, and this noise is larger than the effect of the writer variants.

Under replay, all writer variants scored 0.82--0.85, and two replay runs differed by only 0.005 in
mean score. In the full workflow, a mutant that ignores the research notes scored 0.81, and the base
scored 0.82. Thus, the writer variants differ very little, and the full evaluation cannot settle
these decisions. This test can neither confirm nor reject the replay miniature. Without the
ceiling, the miniature looks invalid.

\subsection{Two limits}
\label{sec:limits-found}
Two shortcuts failed in clear ways. Both failures have one cause: the miniature must imagine
information that only the real system has. These limits are useful, because they tell a team where
not to use a shortcut.

\paragraph{Merged calls.}
One model call wrote the full agent run and imagined each tool result. In W1, this miniature kept
12 of 20 clear decisions. In W3, the planner, the researchers and the writer in one call kept 6 of 8.
But these miniatures missed all 4 mutants that the full workflow caught, and their scores follow
the full scores only weakly ($r = 0.31$ and $0.16$). A limit of two agent steps drops the real
agent from 0.74 to 0.40, but the merged call still scores 0.62. The model imagines the facts that it
did not have the steps to find (Figure~\ref{fig:tracking}c).

\paragraph{Hidden data.}
In W2, the tools read and write a shop database that the simulator cannot see. The simulator
returned a real customer ID in only 22 of 320 runs (real tools: 309 of 320). Box~1 shows an
example. With simulated tools, 260 of 320 runs ended with a hand-over to a human agent, against 63
of 320 with real tools. All variants scored 0.075, so the miniature kept no clear decisions
(Figure~\ref{fig:tracking}d). Stubbed researchers failed in the same way for the W3 planner (0 of
8): the stubs imagine what the planned searches find.

\smallskip\textcolor{simgreen!70!black}{\textbf{Real tool.}} A search by the e-mail address of the customer

\subsection{Diagnoses and fixes}
A miniature is more useful if a team can also learn from its failures. A blind LLM instance read the
failures of one source on 20 development questions and wrote three fixes. Two fix sets from the
same full-workflow failures improved the full workflow by 2.9 and 1.3 points on average. Fixes from
simulated tools improved it by 0.4 points, and fixes from merged calls changed it by $-0.8$ points.
No single fix moved the score by more than two standard errors. Thus, this test is too noisy to
rank the sources.

\paragraph{Another model family.}
A run of W1 on a model of another family, read as a miniature of the main run, kept 13 of 14 clear
decisions ($\tau = 0.27$). Its most frequent failure reason was the same for only 15\% of the
variants, but a different judge graded that run.
