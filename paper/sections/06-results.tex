\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\input{tables/main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares every miniature with the full workflow; Figure~\ref{fig:cost}
plots the trade-off. We read each row against the noise ceiling, the full workflow re-run.

\paragraph{The full workflow settles few decisions.}
Most variants are within noise of the base in every workflow. In W1, two of seventeen variants
differ clearly from the base (caps of two and three agent steps); in W2, two of ten (acting without
confirmation, an eight-step cap); in W3's writer group, only two of five clear pairs survive a re-run. The ceiling rows
make this concrete: re-running the full W1 workflow reproduces its ranking only moderately
(Kendall $\tau=0.67$) but keeps every clear decision (20/20); re-running W3's writer group
reproduces neither ($\tau=-0.05$; 2 of 5 ``clear'' pairs), and re-running its planner group keeps
6 of 8 ($\tau=0.32$). A miniature should therefore be judged by
whether it settles the same \emph{clear} decisions, and by how close it comes to the ceiling on the
rest.

\paragraph{Simulated tools keep decisions, if the simulator can know the answer.}
Replacing tools by an LLM simulator kept 18--19 of 20 clear W1 decisions and every clear decision
about W3's planner (8/8), with rankings close to the ceiling (W1: $\tau=0.54$--$0.61$ against
$0.67$; W3 planner: $0.63$ against the re-run's $0.32$, a gap within noise at eight pairs). Grounding helps: the cache-first simulator, which replays recorded outputs and invents only
unseen ones, beat the pure simulator on every measure, including the judge's reasons ($\pi=0.41$
against $0.27$; ceiling $0.57$). The pure simulator shifts \emph{why} runs fail: retrieval misses
nearly triple, because invented pages lack the facts.
W2 shows the limit, as the fakeability rule (Section~\ref{sec:method}) predicts. Its tools read and
write a private shop database. A pure simulator cannot know which customers, orders and items
exist, so it reported real customers as not found: 260 of 320 simulated runs ended in a hand-over
to a human agent (63 of 320 in the full workflow), every variant scored 0.07, and no clear decision
survived (0 of 14). Tools whose answers depend on hidden state must be replayed, or their simulator
given that state.

\paragraph{Merging calls loses effects that act through tools.}
Running an agent loop as one call that imagines its tool results kept only half of W1's clear
decisions, no better than chance, and caught neither broken variant. Every variant scores about
the same: a two-step cap that drops the real agent from 0.74 to 0.40 scores 0.62 when fused,
because the model imagines the facts it never had the steps to retrieve. The same holds one level
up: stubbing W3's researchers as single imagined calls erased the planner's effect (0/8 clear
pairs; every planner variant 0.59--0.60), and fusing the whole pipeline into one call kept 4 of 8.
In W2 the fused conversation fails outright: the model invents customer and item identifiers, so
its write actions match nothing in the real database and every variant scores 0.07, the share of
tasks that need no change. Fusion measures what the model knows, not how the workflow behaves.

\paragraph{Replay is lossless, and exposes noise in the full evaluation.}
Replaying W3's planner and researchers from the base variant's recording cost 0.21$\times$ and
gave every writer variant identical research. All writer variants then scored 0.82--0.85, and
their ranking disagreed with the full workflow's ($\tau=-0.47$). The ceiling explains why: the
full workflow disagrees with its own re-run just as much, because each full run draws its own
research. With the research held fixed, the writer variants are revealed to differ very little,
which is consistent with the full workflow's own ``ignore the notes'' mutant scoring 0.81 against
the base's 0.82. Without the ceiling, the replay miniature would have been wrongly judged invalid.
For the same reason, the stubbed-researcher miniature's agreement on all five writer pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:cost}) says little: the full workflow itself keeps only two of them.

\paragraph{Fewer tasks and shorter outputs.}
A random quarter of the tasks kept 17 of 20 clear W1 decisions at 0.20$\times$ the cost, and
combines with any other operator. Cutting tool outputs to 1{,}200 characters kept every clear
decision and nearly the ceiling's reason agreement ($\pi=0.54$), but saved little (0.89$\times$):
the agent read slightly more pages (0.91 per run against 0.83).

\paragraph{Diagnoses.}
Keeping decisions does not guarantee keeping diagnoses. In W1, cache-first simulation, shorter
outputs and fewer tasks keep the judge's reasons close to the ceiling ($\pi$ 0.41--0.54 against
0.57); pure simulation keeps decisions but not reasons ($\pi=0.27$); fused miniatures keep neither
($\pi$ 0.13--0.17). Fixes transfer in the same order, though the effects are small: a blind
Claude instance wrote three fixes from each source's failures on 20 development questions. Fixes
from the full workflow improved it by $+2.9$ points on average (two of three helped), fixes from the
cache-first simulator by $+1.3$ (two of three), and fixes from the fused miniature by $-0.8$ (none).
No single fix moved the score by more than two standard errors, so this ordering is a hint, not a
result.

\paragraph{A different model family.}
Read as a miniature of the Gemini run, the OpenAI run of W1 kept 13 of 14 clear decisions
($\tau=0.29$), but its failure reasons differed (the most common reason matched for 15\% of
variants): what fails, and why, depends on the model.
